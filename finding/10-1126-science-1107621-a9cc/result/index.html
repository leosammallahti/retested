Do 15-Month-Old Infants Understand False Beliefs?

Onishi & Baillargeon, 2005 · doi.org/10.1126/science.1107621

← Return to the prediction page

Full source and attempt record

revealed result

Related effect: Infants expect agents to act on false beliefs · FORRT atlas ↗

What people did

Fifteen-month-old babies did a nonverbal task testing whether they could predict an actor's behavior from her belief about where a toy was hidden. Her belief was sometimes true and sometimes false.

What the original found

The babies seemed to expect her to act on what she believed, even when it was wrong.

In the original authors’ words: “15-month-old infants' ability to predict an actor's behavior on the basis of her true or false belief about a toy's hiding place. Results were positive”

What happened when others tried it

3 attempts, oldest first
  1. 2019
    Found itBurnside et al.Developmental Science

    What they did Sixteen-month-old infants watched a toy crane repeatedly move toward a box containing an object. While the crane was out of view, the object changed location. When the crane came back, the researchers measured how long the infants looked at it as it turned toward the object's new location.

    What they found Infants looked longer when the crane turned toward the object's new location, which the authors say is consistent with attributing a false belief to the crane. This is the same looking pattern as in the original study, which used a human actor instead of a crane. The authors suggest infants spontaneously attribute false beliefs to inanimate agents.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “16-month-old infants looked longer when it turned toward the object's new location, consistent with the attribution of a false belief to the crane.”

  2. 2022
    Found part of itLavelleThe British Journal for the Philosophy of Science

    What they did This philosophy of science paper examined attempts to repeat the infant false-belief study, looking at how researchers decided whether a later experiment counted as a repeat of the original.

    What they found The author argues that researchers lack agreed over-arching theories about the concepts under study and the methods used to track them, so they disagree over which experiments count as replications of the original. The paper agrees with an earlier argument that, without agreed theories, a high rate of replication failure is to be expected, but disputes that more replication will resolve the problem.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “. It argues that the lack of consensus about over-arching theories informing both the concepts under study and the methodologies used to track them means that researchers disagree over which experiments constitute replications of the original.”

  3. 2026
    Did not find itPoulin-Dubois & GoldmanCognitive Development

    What they did In two experiments, 16-month-old infants watched an agent who was physically present but could not see an object being moved to a new place: she wore an opaque veil in the first experiment and turned her back in the second. The researchers compared how long infants looked at scenes where the agent's later action matched or did not match what she would believe.

    What they found In both experiments, infants' looking time did not differ significantly between the Congruent and Incongruent conditions, and Bayesian analyses gave moderate support for no difference. These were changed versions of the original setup, not an exact repeat of it. The authors suggest infants may not attribute false beliefs flexibly across contexts, which raises questions about how robust early false-belief understanding is.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Results showed no significant differences in looking time between the Congruent and Incongruent conditions, and Bayesian analyses provided moderate support for the null hypothesis.”

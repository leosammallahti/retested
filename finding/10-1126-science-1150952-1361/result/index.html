Spending Money on Others Promotes Happiness

revealed result

Related effect: Prosocial spending · FORRT atlas ↗

What people did

People were randomly assigned to spend a sum of money either on other people or on themselves. Afterward, they reported how happy they felt.

What the original found

Those told to spend the money on others ended up happier than those told to spend it on themselves.

In the original authors’ words: “participants who were randomly assigned to spend money on others experienced greater happiness than those assigned to spend money on themselves.”

A plain-words account of the experiment, written by an AI model and read against its paper before it was used.

  1. 2014
    Found itGeenenThe Journal of Positive Psychology

    What they did Students were randomly assigned to spend a small amount of money either on themselves or on other people. Half of them received the money as a wage, and the other half received it as a windfall. Afterwards, the students reported how happy they were.

    What they found Students who spent the money on others reported greater happiness than those who spent it on themselves, the same pattern as in the original study. The happiness effect was unaffected by whether the money came as a wage or a windfall.

  3. 2020
    Found part of itAknin et al.Journal of Personality and Social Psychology

    What they did In the first experiment, 712 students were randomly assigned to make a purchase either for themselves or for a stranger in need, then reported their happiness. Two later experiments tested other versions, including recalling a prosocial or personal spending memory; the last had 5,199 people.

    What they found In the first experiment, those assigned to prosocial spending reported greater momentary happiness, as predicted. The second experiment found no benefit for a recall version. In the third, people who recalled a prosocial spending memory reported greater happiness, but the differences were small. The authors conclude spending on others does promote happiness, but the size of the effect depends on methodological features.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “In Experiment 1, 712 students were randomly assigned to make a purchase for themselves or a stranger in need and then reported their happiness. As predicted, participants assigned to engage in prosocial (vs. personal) spending reported greater momentary happiness.”

  4. 2022
    Found part of itKim & DiawPLOS ONE

    What they did Participants were given money and randomly assigned to spend it either on themselves or on someone else. The team then measured how happy they were, using the same analysis as the original experiment, with 133 participants compared with 46 in the original.

    What they found Using the same analysis as the original, the team did not observe a significant difference in happiness between the two groups. An additional analysis, focused on a more direct measure of happiness, did show a significant effect in the direction of the original.

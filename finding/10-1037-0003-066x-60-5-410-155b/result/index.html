revealed result

Related effect: Positive psychology exercises improve happiness and depression · FORRT atlas ↗

What people did

People took part online and were randomly sent to one of six groups. Five groups did a different exercise meant to make people happier. A sixth group did a plausible control exercise.

What the original found

Three of the happiness exercises raised happiness and lowered depressed feelings, and the effects lasted.

In the original authors’ words: “They found that 3 of the interventions lastingly increased happiness and decreased depressive symptoms.”

What happened when others tried it

5 attempts, oldest first
  1. 2012
    Found part of itMongrain & Anselmo-MatthewsJournal of Clinical Psychology

    What they did The team repeated the earlier study's two one-week exercises, "Three Good Things" and "Using your Signature Strengths in a New Way", with the same methods except for an improved control condition. They also added a second "positive placebo" condition, and measured happiness and depression over time.

    What they found The exercises led to lasting increases in happiness, and so did the positive placebo. The exercises did not exceed the control condition in producing changes in depression over time. The original study had found lasting gains in both happiness and depression. The authors suggest such exercises may boost happiness through a common factor, calling up positive information about oneself, rather than specific mechanisms, and that their effects on depression may be more modest than assumed.

  2. 2014
    Did not find itWoodworthUTAS Research Repository

    What they did In the first study, an Australian sample took part in an internet-based randomised trial with four groups: three positive psychology exercises and one control exercise. Each exercise was completed over one week, and happiness and depression were measured, with follow-up measurements taken up to six months afterwards.

    What they found In the first study, happiness rose and depression fell over time in all groups, but there was no differential effect between the positive psychology exercises and the control exercise. The authors report that the original study's findings were not fully replicated.

  4. 2019
    Found part of itKhanna & SinghPsychological Studies

    What they did Adolescents in India took part in positive psychology exercises that lasted one week, including a gratitude visit, activities built on a person's signature strengths, and exercises involving only self-reflection and journaling. Well-being, life satisfaction, happiness and depressive symptoms were measured after the exercises.

    What they found Adolescents who did the gratitude visit and the signature strength exercises reported gains in well-being, life satisfaction and happiness. The self-reflection and journaling exercises had no statistically significant impact on these measures. None of the exercises led to perceived gains in depressive symptoms, which differs from the original study's finding that some interventions decreased them.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

Interventions involving only self-reflection and journaling showed no statistically significant impact on outcome variables. None of the interventions led to perceived gains in scores of depressive symptoms.”

  5. 2020
    Did not find itBasurrah et al.

    What they did The team ran a character strengths intervention for happiness and depression in Saudi Arabia, repeating Seligman et al.'s 2005 study. Participants took part in different conditions, and happiness and depression were measured after the intervention.

    What they found All participants, whichever condition they were in, reported being happier and less depressed after the intervention. The original study had found that three of five interventions, unlike a control exercise, raised happiness and lowered depressive symptoms; here the change appeared in every condition.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words, and read against it by another AI model.

    “all participants, independent of the condition, reported being happier and less depressed (p < .005) post-intervention.”

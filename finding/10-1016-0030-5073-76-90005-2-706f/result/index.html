  3. Did not replicateBiałek et al., 2021Economics and Business Review

    Escalation of commitment is independent of numeracy and cognitive reflection. Failed replication and extension of Staw (1976)

    What they did A total of 365 participants took part in an attempt to repeat Staw's 1976 study of escalation of commitment, the tendency to put more into a failing course of action when you are personally responsible for it. The team also measured each person's numeracy and their cognitive reflection, to test whether those individual differences help explain such behaviour.

    What they found The team found no evidence of escalation of commitment, which does not match the original finding. Participants simply invested more in more promising projects. They also found no evidence that numeracy or cognitive reflection predict escalation behaviour.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “An attempt was made to replicate this finding and test whether individual differences in numeracy and cognitive reflection could help explain such an effect. No evidence for escalation of commitment amongst the participants was found (N = 365). Participants simply invested more in more promising projects. Also, no evidence was found that numeracy or cognitive reflection predict escalation behaviour.”

  4. MixedSchultze & Schulz-Hardt, 2022Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied

    Does change of responsibility reduce escalating commitment? A replication and theoretical extension.

    What they did Across two experiments, the team used a modified version of the escalation task, in which people decide whether to keep investing in a project after bad news. Some decisions were handed to a different person (reassigning responsibility), and the design allowed a check on whether effects lasted beyond a single reinvestment decision and whether the project later recovered or kept failing.

    What they found In the first experiment, they did not find evidence that the effect lasted beyond a single decision. In the second, reassigning responsibility had persistent short-term effects, but these were unspecific: commitment dropped both to projects that kept failing and to ones that ended up profitable. The original finding was therefore only partly reproduced, and the authors question whether reassigning responsibility is an effective intervention against escalating commitment.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “In the first of two experiments, we did not find evidence of temporal stability. || Experiment 2 found persistent short-term effects of responsibility reassignment, but these effects were unspecific, reducing commitment to both losing and ultimately successful courses of action.”

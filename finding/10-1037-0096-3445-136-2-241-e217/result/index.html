Attention control, memory updating, and emotion regulation temporarily reduce the capacity for executive control.

revealed result

Related effects: Attention control reduces subsequent working memory span, Ego Depletion · FORRT atlas ↗

What people did

People did a viewing task and were either told to control where they looked or given no such instructions. Then everyone took a test of how many words they could hold in memory and recall.

What the original found

Those who had controlled their attention recalled fewer words and fewer correct sets of words than those who had not.

In the original authors’ words: “Each of the recall measures offered strong support for the executive depletion hypothesis. Participants who had previously exercised attention control scored worse than participants who had not previously attempted attention control.”

A plain-words account of the experiment, written by an AI model and read against its paper before it was used.

How sure can we be?

what the record holds
  • 3 later attempts, reported in 3 papers, 2011–2016.
  • 1 attempt was preregistered (the plan was filed before the data came in); for the other 2 it is not recorded.
  • Author lists let us check 3 of 3 attempts: 3 were run by teams sharing no author with the original.
  • Pooled across the attempts that report an effect size, the effect is r = .08 (95% range −.23 to .37), against a typical original of r = .21: 37% of the original’s size. (Here r is a scale from 0, no link, to 1, a perfect link.)

What happened when others tried it

3 attempts, oldest first
  1. 2011
    Found itHealey et al.Journal of Experimental Psychology: General

    What they did In the first experiment, 38 participants first did a task that required ignoring words, either a high-demand or a low-demand version. They then did a word-based memory span task (a test of how many words they could recall).

    What they found High-demand participants recalled an average of 54.3% of the words, substantially fewer than the 69.8% recalled by low-demand participants. The authors describe this as replicating the original finding. Across their later experiments, they report that the effect depended on whether the earlier task and the span task used matching material, and they suggest the sensitivity of span to prior tasks does not stem from depleted resources.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “High-demand participants recalled an average of 54.3% {SD = 12.2) of the words, substantially fewer words than the 69.8% (SD = 11.4) recalled by low-demand participants, f(36) = 4.06, p < .001, ci = 1.35, replicating the Schmeichel (2007) finding.”

  2. 2013
    Did not find itCarter & McCulloughBMC Psychology

    What they did A new group of 235 participants did two tasks in a row, either two that demanded high self-control effort or two that demanded less. Between the tasks they swished a drink in their mouth: one with sucrose, or a control drink (unsweetened or sucralose). They then did a working memory test called operation span.

    What they found Participants who swished control drinks showed no evidence of ego depletion, which is worse performance after earlier self-control effort. This does not match the original finding. Among those who swished sucrose, the two effortful tasks improved later performance instead. The authors conclude that claims that self-control failure is caused by the depletion of a resource merit greater circumspection.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “For participants who swished control drinks, we found no evidence of ego depletion.”

  3. 2016
    Did not find itLurquin et al.PLOS ONE

    What they did In a study whose hypotheses, procedure and planned analyses were set down before data collection, two hundred people took part. Some were assigned to a depletion condition and others to a control condition, using a video-viewing attention control task. All then did a second self-control task, and the team also examined task characteristics and individual differences that might change the effect.

    What they found Participants in the depletion condition did not perform worse than control participants on the later self-control task, even after considering those moderator variables. This differs from the original finding that earlier self-control effort worsened later performance. The authors say these findings add to a growing body of evidence suggesting ego-depletion is not a reliable phenomenon, though more research is needed that uses large sample sizes, considers moderator variables, and pre-registers prior to data collection.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Contrary to the ego-depletion hypothesis, participants in the depletion condition did not perform worse than control participants on the subsequent self-control task, even after considering moderator variables.”

Why results may differ

only what a source states or the numbers show
  • The original tested 141 people; the attempts with a recorded size tested between 38 and 235. — FReD
  • Carter & McCullough 2013: “An even more favorable interpretation (albeit, one that ignores the meta-analytic conclusions that we have reported elsewhere; Carter & McCullough 2013) might be that the depletion effect is moderated by the type of experimental task used in the sequential task paradigm— that is, contrary to what was shown by Schmeichel (2007) perhaps OSPAN performance does not decrease when participants are depleted, but …” — the authors’ own words

  1. MixedXiao, 2014Social Psychology

    When More Depletion Offsets the Ego Depletion Effect

    What they did Seventy-two people in a Chinese sample did self-control tasks and were then measured on a later task. Some did one initial self-control task, others did several, and the time spent on self-control was kept equal across conditions.

    What they found After a single initial self-control task, performance on the later task was impaired, the same pattern as the original ego depletion finding. When people did multiple tasks, though, the effect was offset.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Results indicated that although ego depletion occurred after performing a single initial self-control task, engaging in multiple tasks did indeed offset the depletion effect.”

    One initial self-control task impaired later performance, but adding more demanding tasks removed the effect; classify mixed/conditional for the general sequential claim. Chinese sample n=72, self-control duration controlled. Lund primary institutional abstract and cached OpenAlex W2031555777 Psychology.

  2. Did not replicateXu & Trautvetter, 2014PLOS ONE

    Failure to Replicate Depletion of Self-Control

    What they did In four separate studies, two with community samples and two with young adult samples, people first did a baseline self-control measure. They were then randomly given either a depletion task (crossing out letters) or a control task, and did the same self-control measure again. The measures were handgrip perseverance and a modified Stroop. The tasks differed from the original's, so this was a conceptual retest of the original claim.

    What they found None of the four studies showed evidence of a significant depletion effect. The authors say this may mean depletion has more limited effects than prior publications implied, and encourage further replication efforts, particularly among community samples, with full disclosure of positive and negative results.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “There was no evidence for significant depletion effects in any of these four studies.”

    Four fresh randomized studies (two community samples, two young adult samples) used letter-crossing depletion followed by handgrip or Stroop outcomes. This conceptually retests the original sequential self-control impairment claim, although the tasks differ from the original emotion suppression/anagrams paradigm. No original authors overlap.   3. MixedSalmon & de Vet, 2014Frontiers in Psychology

    “When the going gets tough, who keeps going?” Depletion sensitivity moderates the ego-depletion effect

    What they did In the third study, participants did one self-control task and then a second self-control task. The team also looked at how sensitive each person was to depletion (their depletion sensitivity).

    What they found Participants who were sensitive to depletion performed worse on the second self-control task than participants less sensitive to depletion, indicating a stronger ego-depletion effect. The depletion effect was concentrated among those high in depletion sensitivity, so the original's general effect held only under that condition.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

  4. ReplicatedTuk, Zhang & Sweldens, 2015Journal of Experimental Psychology: General

    The propagation of self-control: Self-control in one domain simultaneously improves self-control in other domains

    What they did The team ran 18 studies in which people exerted self-control in one area (controlling attention, food consumption, emotions, or thoughts), then measured self-control in other areas, such as unhealthy food consumption, Stroop task performance and impulsive decision making. In 9 of the studies, the same tasks were also run one after the other.

    What they found Exerting self-control in one area at the same time improved self-control in other areas. When the tasks were run one after the other, the results suggest an ego depletion effect, with a conservative effect size estimate that the authors say is free of data selection and publication biases. They write that this confirms claims that earlier estimates of the effect were inflated by publication bias.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “We provide conservative estimates of the self-control facilitation (d = |0.22|) as well as the ego depletion effect size (d = |0.17|) free of data selection and publication biases.”

    The sequential-task subset directly tests the original effect and yields a small positive estimate d=.17; simultaneous-task studies show the opposite facilitation and are outside this effect scope. Published article, author-disjoint, cached OpenAlex W1977717792 primary Psychology.

    What they did Twenty-three labs, with 2,141 people in total, each ran the same preregistered procedure. People first did one self-control task, then a second task, to test whether the first leaves less self-control for the second. The protocol was based on a sequential-task design by Sripada et al.

    What they found Pooled across the labs, the ego-depletion effect was small, too small to tell apart from no effect at all.

  8. MixedFrancis, 2018Social Psychology

    Development of a Within-Subject, Repeated-Measures Ego-Depletion Paradigm

    What they did Participants went through a series of rounds that alternated depleting tasks with recovery tasks. After each round, the team measured how tired they felt, their mood, and how well they did on a self-control task. The team ran this design across 12 studies with 754 people in total.

    What they found Participants reliably reported lower energy and mood after the depleting tasks than after the recovery tasks. Depleting tasks did not consistently affect behavioral self-control, although the depletion effect was meta-analytically significant when the 12 studies were pooled. Unintended fatigue and practice effects also occurred over the course of the series and systematically interfered with the intended depletion effects.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Depletion manipulations did not consistently affect behavioral self-control, although the depletion effect was meta-analytically significant (d = .045).”

    Twelve within-person experiments, N=754; transient energy/mood lowered reliably, behavioral self-control did not decline consistently, yet pooled behavioral effect d=.045 was statistically significant. Mixed rather than uniform support. Author-hosted published article PDF; OpenAlex W2891578944 Psychology.

  9. Did not replicateVadillo, Gold & Osman, 2018Royal Society Open Science

    Searching for the bottom of the ego well: failure to uncover ego depletion in Many Labs 3

    What they did The team re-examined data from an existing large experiment, Many Labs 3, with more than 2,000 participants. Some people had first completed a Stroop task, a task used in this field to wear down self-control, while others had not. Everyone then worked on anagrams, and the researchers measured how long people kept trying.

    What they found Persistence on the anagram task was not affected by having completed the Stroop task first. This differs from the original claim that an earlier act of self-control reduces later persistence on puzzles; the manipulation and the exact puzzles differed from the original's.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “persistence on an anagram-solving task (a typical measure of self-control) was not affected by previous completion of a Stroop task (a typical depleting task in this literature).”

    Direct conceptual test of the 1998 later-puzzle-persistence claim: randomized prior Stroop vs control and later anagram persistence in >2,000 Many Labs 3 participants. This article is a secondary analysis of an existing large experiment, not independent new recruitment; manipulation and exact puzzle form differ. Null result.   12. MixedRadel, Gruet & Barzykowski, 2019PLOS ONE

    Testing the ego-depletion effect in optimized conditions

    What they did This test of the ego-depletion idea used an improved protocol: a well-learned Simon task done before and after 1 hour of Stroop practice, with 75% incongruent trials (high inhibition demands) or none (the control). In Study 1 (82 people) each person did one condition; in Study 2 (52 people) each did both.

    What they found In Study 1, the high-demand condition led to a greater interference effect and accuracy decline on the later task than the control condition, as the ego-depletion idea predicts. In Study 2, no significant ego-depletion effect was found. Because it is difficult to explain this difference, the authors say that even in conditions chosen to optimize the effect, it seems difficult to be conclusive about whether it exists.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “A significant ego-depletion effect was found in Study 1 (greater interference effect and accuracy decline in high inhibition demands than in control condition) but not in Study 2.”

    Independent conceptual replication of the general two-task ego-depletion effect, using prolonged Stroop inhibition followed by Simon-task performance. It does not repeat any particular 1998 task sequence. Later paper explicitly cites the 1998 original (PubMed reference 2). Between-subjects Study 1 supported the effect; within-subjects Study 2 did not. Later OpenAlex W2921388073 primary field Psychology.

  14. MixedLin & Friese, 2020Psychological Science

    Strong Effort Manipulations Reduce Response Caution: A Preregistered Reinvention of the Ego-Depletion Paradigm

    What they did Across four preregistered experiments with 686 people in total, each person did a task that demanded a high level of effort and then did a Stroop task (naming the ink colour of a word while ignoring what the word says), which measures how well people hold back an automatic response. The researchers also analysed how cautious people were in their responses.

    What they found The effort manipulation reduced how cautious people were in responding. It did not affect their later performance on the Stroop task, whether measured by traditional Stroop behavioural measures or by additional diffusion-model analyses. The original idea was that high initial effort would impair later task performance, so this matches it only in part.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “However, our demand manipulation did not affect subsequent inhibition, as assessed with traditional Stroop behavioral measures and additional diffusion-model analyses for conflict tasks.”

    Four preregistered within-subjects N=686 conceptual tests: high initial effort changed response caution but did not impair subsequent Stroop inhibition. Mixed because process-level decision caution moved while original task-performance prediction failed. Psychology-primary.

  15. MixedPrimoceri, 2021Journal of Experimental Social Psychology

    The role of task similarity for ego depletion: A registered report

    What they did The team ran a preregistered pilot with 80 people and a registered replication with 300. Everyone first did either a visual stop-signal task or a lexical control task. They then did a second task that was either similar to the first or dissimilar from it.

    What they found The predicted crossover pattern appeared: similarity between the two tasks changed how people did on the second task, depending on which first task they had done. The authors report that evidence for facilitation from similar tasks was stronger than evidence for depletion from dissimilar tasks.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

  16. MixedMangin & Benraiss, 2021Psychology of Sport and Exercise

    No ego-depletion effect without a good control task

    What they did In the first of three experiments, people did a 30-minute modified Stroop task, which taps inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility, or a control task of reading color words. Afterwards they did a handgrip endurance task. In the third experiment, the control task was watching a documentary instead.

    What they found In the first experiment, the ego-depletion effect was not replicated, and the color-word reading control task induced boredom and subjective fatigue. In the third experiment, with the documentary as the control, the effect was replicated. The authors conclude that the choice of control task is crucial and that it appears necessary to control for boredom in future replications.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “In the first study, we compared the performance of a handgrip endurance task after this depleting task or after a classic control task (i.e., reading color words) and failed to replicate the ego-depletion effect. We showed that the control task induced boredom and subjective fatigue. In a second study, we looked for a better control task and compared the color-word reading task to another possible control task used…”

    Independent conceptual replication of the Baumeister et al. 1998 sequential two-task ego-depletion effect, explicitly cited in later full text. Experiment 1 (Stroop vs boring color-word control then handgrip) failed; Experiment 3 (Stroop vs documentary control then handgrip) succeeded. Different task sequence than 1998 and control-task boredom materially changes result. Untrusted pool linked to a 2012 review instead of empirical original; corrected to Baumeister et al. 1998.   18. ReplicatedTroll, Friese & Loschelder, 2022British Journal of Social Psychology

    What they did A total of 560 participants did two tasks in a row. The first task was either high-demand or low-demand, and in some conditions money was offered for charity if they put in effort on it. The second task measured self-control performance.

    What they found In the conditions with no incentive, self-control performance was impaired after the high-demand task compared with the low-demand task, the standard ego depletion effect. The incentives had an unexpected effect: participants showed higher intrinsic, autonomous motivation but did not exert greater effort, which limited the registered predictions comparing the two models, so the authors tested updated predictions.

  22. Did not replicatede Luca, 2025Experimental Psychology

    Ego Does Not Deplete Over Time

    What they did In an online experiment, participants switched between blocks of a numerical Stroop task (a task in which they respond to numbers while ignoring conflicting information) and a Global-Local Task (used to measure the impact of depletion). The Stroop blocks had either 50% or 10% incongruent trials, making one version more demanding than the other. The team predicted worse Global-Local performance after the more demanding version, with the cost growing over time on task.

    What they found The classical Stroop and global-local effects appeared. But the team found no evidence that their manipulation produced an outcome that can be considered evidence for ego depletion, so the original finding was not found. The authors say their findings add to the literature questioning the robustness of ego-depletion effects under certain task conditions.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “we found no evidence that our experimental manipulation successfully induced an outcome that can be considered as evidence for ego depletion.”

    Independent online numerical Stroop to global-local sequential test, with task duration prediction; no depletion evidence though Stroop/global-local task effects appeared. PubMed publisher-deposited abstract and OpenAlex W4410968618 Psychology.

Self-control relies on glucose as a limited energy source: Willpower is more than a metaphor

Gailliot et al., 2007 · doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.2.325

← Return to the prediction page

Full source and attempt record

revealed result

Related effects: Self-control relies on glucose effect, Ego Depletion · FORRT atlas ↗

What people did

People did a task that took self-control, then did a second one. Before the second task, some drank a glucose drink, which is sugar the body uses for energy.

What the original found

Self-control in the first task made people do worse on the second, but a glucose drink erased that drop.

In the original authors’ words: “initial acts of self-control impaired performance on subsequent self-control tasks, but consuming a glucose drink eliminated these impairments.”

A plain-words account of the experiment, written by an AI model and read against its paper before it was used.

Did it hold up?
Largely failed

Most later attempts did not find it.

Of 8 later attempts, 2 found part of it, 6 did not find it.

How sure can we be?

what the record holds
  • 8 later attempts, reported in 8 papers, 2012–2016.
  • Pooled across the attempts that report an effect size, the effect is r = .11 (95% range −.07 to .28), against a typical original of r = .51: 21% of the original’s size. (Here r is a scale from 0, no link, to 1, a perfect link.)

  2. 2012
    Did not find itMolden & ScholerPsychological Science

    What they did Across four experiments, the team compared ingesting a carbohydrate solution with rinsing the mouth with it without ingesting it, and looked at the effect on later self-control. The study set effects of oral sensing of carbohydrates (a motivational account) against effects of glucose ingestion (a metabolic account). Blood glucose was also measured.

    What they found Swallowing carbohydrates did not improve later self-control, which differs from the original finding that a glucose drink eliminated the impairment. Rinsing the mouth with the carbohydrate solution, without swallowing it, did immediately bolster self-control, and it did so without changing blood glucose.

  3. 2013
    Found part of itJobProceedings of the National Academy of Sciences

    What they did In three experiments, people did a demanding task and then a later self-control task. Their beliefs about willpower were measured or manipulated, and glucose was given in some conditions.

    What they found Only people who view willpower as limited and easily depleted (a limited resource theory) showed improved self-control after sugar consumption. Glucose had no such effect for people with nonlimited beliefs. This matches the original finding only for people holding limited-willpower beliefs, which runs against the original's broader claim that self-control relies on glucose as a limited energy source.

  4. 2014
    Did not find itLangeAppetite

    What they did In the first experiment, 70 people drank either a sugar drink or a drink with a non-caloric sweetener between two rounds of delay-discounting tasks, which test self-control. In the second, 115 people rinsed their mouths with glucose, and the team varied the time between the rinse and the next self-control task.

    What they found Neither experiment found that sugar helped. Blood glucose rose after the sugar drink in the first experiment, yet performance did not differ between groups. The original had found a glucose drink eliminated the impairment.

  5. 2014
    Did not find itLange et al.Journal of European Psychology Students

    What they did A recent replication, in which the first and second self-control tasks were identical, indicated that the effect of sugar on ego depletion had been substantially overestimated. This study tested whether that result holds up with dissimilar tasks. Seventy participants did one self-control task and then a second, different self-control task. Between the two tasks, each drank either a sugar-containing drink or one with an artificial sweetener.

    What they found The results suggest that sugar consumption does not counteract ego depletion, the drop in self-control after an earlier self-control task. This did not match the original finding that a glucose drink eliminated that drop. The authors report the result held even though the two tasks were dissimilar.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Results suggest that sugar consumption does not counteract ego depletion even when dissimilar self-control tasks are used.”

  6. 2015
    Did not find itKellyPLOS ONE

    What they did In a double-blind experiment, 67 people first did a Stroop task, a self-control exercise. They then received either a glucose drink or a placebo and did a second self-control task, the antisaccade task. This second task differs from the one used in the original work.

    What they found Glucose did not improve antisaccade performance after the Stroop task. Participants with high trait self-control showed improvement on some aspects of the task, a secondary subgroup result. The overall result differs from the original finding that a glucose drink eliminated the impairment.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Glucose did not enhance antisaccade performance following self-control exertion in the Stroop task.”

  7. 2015
    Did not find itBoyle & AllenPhysiology & Behavior

    What they did Forty older adults each took part in a crossover design. After a task meant to use up their self-control, they either swallowed or rinsed their mouths with a sucrose drink or a sucralose drink, then did a self-control task.

    What they found No significant effects of ingesting or rinsing sucrose on self-control were observed. This differs from the original finding that a glucose drink eliminated the impairment after an earlier self-control task. The later study used a different, attention-switch self-control task and a different age group (older adults) from the original.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “no significant effects of ingesting or rinsing sucrose on self-control were observed.”

  8. 2016
    Found part of itStanding et al.Psychology Teaching Review

    What they did Four teams of five undergraduates each independently attempted exact replications of Study 8 of the original work. In all, 306 participants first drank either a glucose drink or a placebo, then wrote about death (meant to use up self-control) or about dental pain (the control). Self-control was measured by how many items they left unsolved in a word puzzle.

    What they found The glucose drink raised self-control overall, as in the original, though absolute self-control levels were lower than in the original. The glucose effect occurred only with female participants, and the death-writing task had no effect. The four teams reported similar results to one another. The authors suggest that this use of parallel student teams offers pedagogical benefits for both students and teachers, potentially contributing to a resolution of the replication crisis in psychology.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “its main result was replicated, since self–control overall was raised by the lucose drink.”

  • Kelly 2015: Conceptual retest with different second task from Gailliot. — reviewer’s note
  • Boyle & Allen 2015: Different later attention-switch task and age group from Gailliot. — reviewer’s note

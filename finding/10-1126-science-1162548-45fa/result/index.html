Experiencing Physical Warmth Promotes Interpersonal Warmth

Williams & Bargh, 2008 · doi.org/10.1126/science.1162548

← Return to the prediction page

Full source and attempt record

revealed result

Related effect: Physical warmth promotes interpersonal warmth · FORRT atlas ↗

What people did

People held either a hot or a cold therapeutic pad. Afterward they could pick a gift, either for a friend or for themselves.

What the original found

The people who held the hot pad were more likely to choose the gift for a friend than the people who held the cold pad.

In the original authors’ words: “participants who briefly held a cup of hot (versus iced) coffee judged a target person as having a "warmer" personality”

A plain-words account of the experiment, written by an AI model and read against its paper before it was used.

Did it hold up?
Largely failed

Most later attempts did not find it.

  3. 2014
    Did not find itLynott et al.Social Psychology

    What they did Three independent labs each had participants evaluate either a hot or a cold instant therapeutic pack. Afterwards, participants chose a reward for taking part, either a treat for a friend or a treat for themselves.

    What they found The hot pack did not make participants more likely to pick the treat for a friend. This held in each lab on its own and when the three labs' results (861 people in total) were combined. The original study had found that people holding the hot pack were more likely to choose a gift for a friend. The authors conclude there is no evidence that brief exposure to warm therapeutic packs induces greater prosocial responding than exposure to cold packs.

  7. 2017
    Did not find itLynottArchives of Scientific Psychology

    What they did A sample of 611 people took part. Some held a hot or cold therapeutic pack, and the room's temperature was also recorded. The team looked at whether the pack's temperature or the ambient temperature went with more prosocial than selfish choices. The participants were a subsample of those recruited for an earlier registered replication.

    What they found Warmer ambient temperatures were weakly associated with more prosocial than selfish choices, but the authors say the evidence is better described as inconclusive. There was no indication that perceiving hot or cold temperatures with the hands predicted a reliable increase in prosocial behavior, which does not match the original gift-choice finding. The prop-temperature result agrees with previously published analyses of the same data set, and the authors say it fails to support predictions from particular accounts of social embodiment.

  9. 2018
    Did not find itChabris et al.

    What they did The team repeated both of the original experiments in field settings, using double-blind procedures. In the first, 128 participants took part, and in the second, 177. The experiments tested whether holding a hot cup of coffee changes how warm someone judges a person's personality, and whether holding a therapeutic heat pad changes whether people choose rewards for others rather than for themselves.

    What they found In both experiments the team found near-zero effects. The original studies had reported large effects, with 41 and 53 participants. The authors' Bayesian analyses suggest substantially more evidence for no effect than for the original physical warmth priming idea.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

  11. 2019
    Did not find itChabris & MandartSocial Psychology

    What they did The team repeated both of the original experiments in field settings, with 128 people in the first and 177 in the second. In the first, people held a hot cup of coffee and then judged a person's personality as warmer or not. In the second, people held a therapeutic heat pad and chose rewards for other people or for themselves. The procedures were double-blind.

    What they found The team found near-zero effects in both experiments. The original had reported that physical warmth shifted these judgments and choices; this was not found here. The original experiments had small samples (41 and 53 participants) and barely statistically significant results; Bayesian analyses suggested substantially more evidence for the null hypothesis of no effect than for the original physical warmth priming hypothesis.

Power Posing

Carney et al., 2010 · doi.org/10.1177/0956797610383437

← Return to the prediction page

Full source and attempt record

revealed result

Related effects: Power Posing, Expansive poses raise testosterone and lower cortisol, Power poses increase risk tolerance, Expansive poses raise felt power · FORRT atlas ↗

What people did

People held two body poses for about a minute each. Some took wide, open, take-up-space poses, and others took closed, shrunken-in poses. Afterward they could take a gamble or play it safe.

What the original found

More of the open-pose group took the gamble than the closed-pose group.

In the original authors’ words: “As hypothesized, high-power poses caused an increase in testosterone compared with low-power poses, which caused a decrease in testosterone, F(1, 39) = 4.29, p < .05; r = .34”

A plain-words account of the experiment, written by an AI model and read against its paper before it was used.

Did it hold up?
Largely failed

Most later attempts did not find it.

Of 15 later attempts, 1 found it, 4 found part of it, 10 did not find it.

each mark is one later attempt

How sure can we be?

what the record holds
  • 15 later attempts, reported in 15 papers, 2013–2026.
  • Of the 3 attempts with a recorded answer, 2 were preregistered (the plan was filed before the data came in); for the other 12 it is not recorded.
  • Author lists let us check 1 of 15 attempts: 1 was run by a team sharing no author with the original.
  • The coding sources disagree on 1 attempt; those are marked below.
  • Pooled across the attempts that report an effect size, the effect is r = .07 (95% range .01 to .12), against a typical original of r = .30: 22% of the original’s size. (Here r is a scale from 0, no link, to 1, a perfect link.)
  • Each verdict is the replicating authors’ own conclusion, recorded by FORRT’s coders or checked by Retested reviewers against the later paper.

What happened when others tried it

  1. 2013
    Found part of itCesario & McDonaldSocial Cognition

    What they did The later paper ran two experiments on whether holding expansive or constrictive body poses changes how powerful people feel. In the first, poses were held either with or without an interpersonal context. In the second, people were given roles that carried information about what they could do.

    What they found In the first experiment, expansive and constrictive poses influenced power only when held in an interpersonal context, which gives the poses meaning as dominance and submissiveness. In the second, poses had no effect on power when roles provided more important information about potential action. This matches the original pose-power link only under some conditions; the study tested felt power only, not the original's hormone or risk-taking results.

    What they did The later team tested 247 men after a physical competition, so some were winners and some were losers. The men then held a pose, and the team measured their testosterone and cortisol, their risk-taking, and their feelings of power.

The original study had reported that high-power poses raised testosterone, lowered cortisol, and increased feelings of power and tolerance for risk.     Did not find itGarrison et al.Social Psychological and Personality Science

    What they did A total of 305 participants held either an expansive (high power) or contractive (low power) body pose. While holding it, they gazed either straight ahead or down at the ground. Afterward they played a hypothetical ultimatum game, made a gambling decision, and reported how powerful and in charge they felt.

    What they found Neither body posture nor eye gaze influenced the gambling decision. Contrary to the original's prediction, adopting an expansive pose reduced feelings of power. Holding a direct gaze did increase the probability of rejecting a low offer on the ultimatum game.

    Did not find itRonay et al.Comprehensive Results in Social Psychology

    What they did In the Netherlands, participants were put into high-power or low-power physical postures. The team measured their testosterone, cortisol and risk-taking, and also tested whether overconfidence and testosterone linked posing to risk-taking.

    What they found The team was unable to replicate the original findings and found no evidence for their extended hypotheses. Overconfidence was unaffected by power posing and unrelated to testosterone, cortisol, and risk-taking. They raise the possibility that cultural differences may play a moderating role.

  6. 2017
    Did not find itKeller et al.

    What they did Participants were put through power poses, and the team directly varied whether they knew what the poses were meant to do. They then measured power in the traditional ways and with a more ecologically valid assessment.

    What they found The team found no evidence for an overall effect of power poses, and none for an interaction with awareness, on either the traditional measures of power or the more ecologically valid assessment. The original study reported that power poses changed hormones, feelings of power and risk tolerance. The authors say the results suggest the benefits of using power poses outside laboratory settings are limited.

  7. 2017
    Did not find itBailey, LaFrance & DovidioComprehensive Results in Social Psychology

    What they did Male and female participants looked at images of male and female models in low-power and high-power postures and were asked to enact each posture for an unspecific duration. The team measured how long people held the posture, their risky behavior, and how powerful they felt. The study was preregistered.

    What they found The preregistered prediction that posture and participant gender would interact was not supported. Overall, participants enacted the high-power postures longer than the low-power posture, and only male participants' time in the postures depended on the model's gender. The authors write that they "did not strongly replicate prior work," though exploratory analyses of some parts of the sample found some support that high-power postures led to riskier behavior and more felt power.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

  8. 2017
    Did not find itLatu et al.Comprehensive Results in Social Psychology

    What they did Two hundred participants were randomly assigned to hold either an open or a closed body posture while reading either a strong or a weak persuasive message about taxing junk food. Afterwards they reported their attitudes toward junk food, how powerful they felt, how confident they were in their thoughts, and how open they felt.

    What they found Open versus closed posture did not change how powerful people felt, so the earlier effect of posture on subjective power was not replicated. Posture did not affect any of the other measures either. Strong messages, compared with weak ones, led to more persuasion, higher subjective power, more thought confidence and more openness. The authors say this challenges the idea of a direct causal link between open postures and feelings of power.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Results failed to replicate the previously found effect of body posture on subjective feelings of power.”

  12. 2019
    Did not find itMetzler & GrèzesPeerJ

    What they did 82 men took a face-sorting task in which they categorized faces based on first impressions. In between blocks of the task, each man held an expansive or a constrictive posture for 2 minutes, and this was repeated. Saliva samples were taken at two time points to measure testosterone, cortisol and progesterone.

    What they found Testosterone and cortisol, which are linked to dominance behaviors, did not respond differently to expansive as opposed to constrictive postures. Neither did progesterone, which is related to affiliative tendencies. The original study had reported higher testosterone and lower cortisol after power postures, and this was not found.

  13. 2020
    Found itKörnerSchool Psychology International

    What they did Fourth-grade children performed either high-power poses or low-power poses. The study was preregistered. Afterwards the children were measured on their feelings, including how powerful they felt, and on how they saw their relationship with their teacher.

    What they found Children who had performed high-power poses, compared with those who had performed low-power poses, mentioned more positive feelings, higher power feelings, and a more positive student–teacher relationship. On this indirect measure of feelings, this matches the original's finding of increased feelings of power. The study did not test the original's hormone or risk-taking measures.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

  14. 2024
    Found part of itBarel et al.BMC Psychology

    What they did Ninety-two young adults (30 males, 32 females taking oral contraceptives, and 30 females not taking them who were in their midluteal phase) were randomly assigned to a high-power-pose or a low-power-pose condition. They reported their feelings of power and did a risk-taking task. Hormone levels were measured before and after the pose manipulation.

    What they found Power posing did not replicate the original results for testosterone levels or feelings of power. The findings partially supported the original results for cortisol levels and risk-taking: among high-power posers, a decrease in cortisol was associated with risk tolerance. The team also found that, among females taking oral contraceptives, high-power posing increased estradiol levels; the authors call these findings preliminary.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Power posing was not found to replicate the original results regarding effects on testosterone levels or feelings of power; however, our findings partially supported the original results regarding effects on cortisol levels and risk-taking.”

  15. 2026
    Found part of itWainio-Theberge & ArmonyBritish Journal of Psychology

    What they did Participants did a 30-trial balloon task, a risk-taking game, while their posture was shaped by the environment rather than by instructed poses: table height led to an upright or a stooped posture. The team also measured mood.

    What they found There was no significant difference between the posture groups overall. But the groups differed in how their risk-taking changed across the repeated trials. This study was not preregistered. The original found that posing in expansive postures raised tolerance for risk; here the effect showed up over repeated trials, and against a separately recruited control group only the expansive posture differed.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “There was a simple main effect of trial (β = 0.37, χ² = 39.5, p < .0001), as well as an interaction between trial and group (β = 0.26, χ² = 5.02, p = .03); the simple main effect of group did not reach significance”
    paperfree text

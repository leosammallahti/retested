Result record

Unskilled and unaware of it: How difficulties in recognizing one's own incompetence lead to inflated self-assessments.

Kruger & Dunning, 1999 · doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.6.1121

← Return to the prediction page

Full source and attempt record

revealed result

Related effect: Dunning-Kruger effect

What people did

People took tests of humor, grammar and logic and estimated how well they had done. Those who scored in the bottom quarter, around the 12th percentile, put themselves around the 62nd.

What the original found

This was linked to a weaker ability to tell accurate answers from errors, and improving their skills helped them see their limits.

In the original authors’ words: “Across 4 studies, the authors found that participants scoring in the bottom quartile on tests of humor, grammar, and logic grossly overestimated their test performance and ability.”

  2. 2013
    Found itParkKorean Journal of Social and Personality Psychology

    What they did Undergraduate students in a university course took their final exam. Right after it, they estimated how well they had done. The team compared each student's estimate with their actual score, and also looked at how much effort students had put into preparing.

    What they found The team observed the Dunning-Kruger effect, the same pattern the original study reported: the less competent students overestimated their performance. Effort in preparing raised actual scores, which reduced overestimation, but also directly increased it. The explanation that the effect is an artifact of perceived difficulty was not supported. The authors say the results fit the original metacognition explanation better than the regression-to-the-mean or task-difficulty explanations.

  3. 2015
    Found part of itKim, Chiu & BregantPLOS ONE

    What they did The team ran a visual-pun task and varied how much the task was presented as relevant to the participants themselves (high or low self-relevance). They looked at whether people who performed poorly overestimated how well they had done.

    What they found As the authors predicted, the pattern of low performers overestimating themselves was attenuated when the task was perceived to have low self-relevance.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “As predicted, the unskilled and unaware effect was attenuated when the task was perceived to have low self-relevance.”

  5. 2019
    Found part of itMcIntoshJournal of Experimental Psychology: General

    What they did The team ran a preregistered test of the same idea using movement tasks and spatial-memory tasks. They measured how well people did, how well they judged their own performance, and how well they could tell their own successes from errors (metacognitive measures).

    What they found These metacognitive measures correlated with skill but contributed weakly to the pattern of overestimating one's own performance. When task success was equalized, the pattern disappeared. The authors present a model in which metacognitive differences can contribute to the effect but are neither necessary nor sufficient for it.

  8. 2021
    Found itLeman, Kurinec & RowattThe Journal of Positive Psychology

    What they did In two studies, people took tests of fluid intelligence and of general knowledge, and estimated how well they had done. They were also measured on intellectual humility.

    What they found Low performers tended to over-estimate their performance, as in the original finding. People who were less intellectually humble were more susceptible to this over-estimation.

  9. 2021
    Found itTremayneInternational Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology

    What they did The team tested 83 men on hand-grip strength, a physical ability rather than a mental one. The men's own estimates of their performance were compared with how they actually did.

    What they found The worst performers were the most miscalibrated and significantly overestimated themselves, whereas the best performers significantly underestimated themselves. The authors describe this as consistent with the typical Dunning-Kruger effect found in the original study.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Consistent with the typical Dunning-Kruger effect, it was found that the worst performers were the most miscalibrated and significantly overestimated themselves, whereas the best performers significantly underestimated themselves.”

  10. 2022
    Found part of itHoferJournal of Intelligence

    What they did A group of 281 people took part. The team compared how people rated their own general, verbal, and spatial intelligence with how they actually did. They looked at whether those with lower ability knew less about where they stood.

    What they found Standard analyses showed the pattern in general, verbal, and spatial intelligence. With improved statistical methods, only verbal intelligence still showed some support: people with lower verbal intelligence tended to have less self-knowledge about it. So the original pattern held in part.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “While standard analyses indicated Dunning-Kruger effects in general, verbal, and spatial intelligence, improved statistical methods only yielded some support for one in verbal intelligence: people with lower verbal intelligence tended to have less self-knowledge about it.”
    paperfree text
  11. 2023
    Found itPilottiBehavioral Sciences

    What they did University students took exams, and the team looked at how well their estimates of their own test grades matched their actual grades, including estimates made after completing the final test. They also asked how confident students were in those estimates.

    What they found Students who performed poorly overestimated their test grades, the same pattern as in the original study. Their estimates improved after they completed the final test, and they held those estimates with little confidence. The authors favored wishful thinking, rather than unawareness of being unprepared, as the explanation.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “In fact, although they overestimated their test grades, their estimates not only improved after completing the final test but also were regarded with little confidence.”
    paperfree text

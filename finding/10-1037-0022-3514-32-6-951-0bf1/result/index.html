What people did

Men guessed heads or tails for a series of coin tosses. Some got many right early and fewer later, others did worse early and better later, and others had a random mix of hits and misses.

What the original found

Those who got the early guesses right rated themselves as better at predicting coin tosses than the other two groups did.

In the original authors’ words: “Ss in the descending condition rated themselves as significantly better at predicting the outcomes of coin tosses than Ss in either of the other 2 groups.”

What happened when others tried it

2 attempts, oldest first
  1. 2022
    Found itEben et al.International Gambling Studies

    What they did An online sample of 289 healthy volunteers, recruited through crowdsourcing, took part in a coin-tossing task. Each person saw one of three sequences of wins and losses: more wins at the start, more losses at the start, or a random order. Afterwards they estimated how well they could predict the outcome of a coin toss.

    What they found People shown more wins at the start rated their ability to predict coin tosses higher than people shown more losses at the start or a random sequence. This matches the original finding, though the effect sizes were small to medium and smaller than in the original study.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Thus, we replicated the findings in an online context, although the effect size was smaller than expected.”

  2. 2022
    Found itEben et al.International Gambling Studies

    What they did In two online experiments, 600 crowdsourced participants each took part in a coin-tossing task. Three groups saw different sequences of outcomes: more wins at the start, more losses at the start, or a random sequence. Participants estimated how well they could predict the coin toss, and their response speed after wins and losses was measured.

    What they found As in the original, participants shown more wins at the beginning rated their ability to predict the coin toss higher than those shown more losses at the beginning or a random sequence. Participants responded faster after losses than after wins, but the illusion of control did not change this, which the authors say is inconsistent with several theoretical accounts.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “We replicated that participants presented with more wins at the beginning of a sequence estimated their ability to predict the outcome of a coin-toss higher than participants presented with more losses at the beginning, or those presented with a random sequence.”

    Outcome sequences and illusion of control - Part I: An online replication of Langer & Roth (1975)

    “Thus, we replicated the findings in an online context, although the effect size was smaller than expected.”

    Independent preregistered online coin-toss experiment, N=289. The descending early-win sequence produced higher control ratings than ascending or random sequences, matching the exact original effect with smaller magnitude. Authors disjoint. Online publication 2022, journal issue 2023.

  2. ReplicatedEben et al., 2022International Gambling Studies

    Outcome sequences and illusion of control – part II: the effect on post-loss speeding

    “We replicated that participants presented with more wins at the beginning of a sequence estimated their ability to predict the outcome of a coin-toss higher than participants presented with more losses at the beginning, or those presented with a random sequence. Additionally, participants generally responded more quickly after a loss than after a win. However, the illusion of control did not influence post-loss…”

    Distinct from the same team's Part I N=289 paper already curated: Part II reports two new online experiments of N=600 each, retesting Langer and Roth's early-win confidence rating effect before examining a different post-loss-speeding question. The early-win effect replicated; the null effect on post-loss speed is a separate extension and is not part of this pair's outcome. Online 2022, issue 2023. Authors disjoint from original. Later OpenAlex cached primary Psychology field. FLoRA marks the whole Part II article mixed because its new post-loss-speeding extension did not show the hypothesized effect; the source-backed primary early-win confidence comparison replicated and is the only effect scoped here.

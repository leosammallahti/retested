Color red on attractiveness

The claim

Seeing a person in red, or against a red background, makes them look more attractive and sexually appealing to potential romantic partners.

In the original authors' words: “an independent-samples t test exam-ining the influence of color condition on perceived attractiveness revealed a significant color effect, t(32) 2.44, p .05, d 0.86. Participants in the red condition, compared with those in the gray condition, rated the…”

Tested the original paper

Romantic red: Red enhances men's attraction to women.

Elliot & Niesta · 2008 · doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.95.5.1150

In five experiments, researchers compared how men viewed women in connection with the color red versus other colors. Red led men to see women as more attractive and more sexually desirable, and men seemed unaware of it. Red did not change women's views of other women's attractiveness, or men's views of women's likeability, kindness or intelligence.

  2. 2016
    Did not find itPeperkoorn et al.

    What they did In the first experiment, Dutch men rated a woman shown in red, white, and black on how attractive and how sexually attractive she was. The second experiment repeated this with an American sample. In the third, the team repeated an earlier study that had found a red effect, using a substantially larger sample.

    What they found Across the three studies, totaling 830 men, none supported the red effect: the woman in red was not rated as more attractive. This differs from the original finding that red made men view women as more attractive and more sexually desirable.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, and read against it by another AI model.

Tested the original paper

Red, rank, and romance in women viewing men.

Elliot et al. · 2010 · Journal of Experimental Psychology: General · doi.org/10.1037/a0019689

Study 3

Women looked at a photograph of a man shown on either a red or a gray background and rated how attractive he was. They rated him as more attractive on the red background.

  1. 2014
    Did not find itBanas

    What they did A group of six students at the University of Edinburgh ran a direct replication of one of the original experiments (Experiment 3), using exactly the same materials and a similar sample of undergraduate university students.

    What they found The students did not find the original effects. They note that, unlike the original, their sample was made up of students in the UK, and that the study was run alongside another colour-related replication, with the red and romance study always run first.

  3. 2015
    Did not find itHesslinger et al.Psychonomic Bulletin & Review

    What they did In the first experiment, a close copy of the original, 89 participants (72 of them women) saw the same photograph of a young man on either a red or a white background and rated how attractive he looked. In the second, 32 participants rated photos of several men wearing formal or casual clothing.

    What they found Red had no significant impact on attractiveness ratings in either experiment, unlike the original finding. In the second experiment, men in formal clothing were rated as more attractive than men in casual clothing. The authors say their results question the robustness and the ecological validity of the original finding.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “We did not find any significant impact of red in either of the studies.”

  7. 2019
    Did not find itWagge et al.Collabra: Psychology

    What they did Undergraduate students ran planned repeats of one experiment. Women looked at photographs of men with red borders, and the students measured how attractive the women rated them, both in general (perceived attractiveness) and sexually (sexual attractiveness). The nine projects, with 640 people in total, were pre-registered and reviewed by experts.

    What they found Pooled together, the projects found no effect of red on either kind of attractiveness rating. This did not match the original finding, and the result was the same with or without the original study's data included.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Results of this meta-analysis showed no effect of red on attractiveness ratings for either perceived attractiveness (mean ratings difference = –0.07, 95% CI [–0.31, 0.16]) or sexual attractiveness (mean ratings difference = –0.06, 95% CI [–0.36, 0.24]); this null result held with and without Elliot et al.’s (2010) data included in analyses.”

Effects / Psychology

A fixed saving matters more on a cheap than an expensive item

Tversky & Kahneman, 1981

The claim

People are more willing to make a trip to save the same few dollars on a cheap item than on a pricey one.

In the original authors' words: “68 percent of the respondents were willing to make an extra trip to save $5 on a $15 calculator; only 29 percent were willing to exert the same effort when the price of the calculator was $125.”

How the original study workedChanges the task or its wordingmeasured as What people actually did or chose

What people did

Study 1

People chose between two programs where lives were at stake. One group read: 200 saved for sure, or a one-in-three chance of saving 600. Another read: 400 die for sure, or a one-in-three chance nobody dies.

What the original found

Most took the sure thing when it was about lives saved, and the gamble when it was about deaths.

In the original authors’ words: “Choices involving gains are often risk-averse, while choices involving losses are often risk-seeking. For example, in Problem 1 (N = 152), 72% of participants chose the safe option (Program A: 200 lives saved, p < .05), compared to 28% who chose the risky option (Program B: 1/3 probability of …”

Study 10

People imagined they were about to buy a calculator and could drive to another store to save $5 on it. For some, the calculator cost $15. For others, it cost $125.

What the original found

Most people would make the extra trip to save $5 on the $15 calculator (68%), but far fewer would for the $125 one (29%).

In the original authors’ words: “The response to the two versions of problem 10 were markedly different: 68 percent of the respondents were willing to make an extra trip to save $5 on a $15 calculator; only 29 percent were willing to exert the same effort when the price of the calculator was $125.”

    Found itSprengholzBehavior Research Methods

    What they did Participants answered questions through a voice-controlled virtual assistant (Google Assistant), using either a smartphone or a smart speaker. The study repeated the framing experiment, in which the same decision problem is worded in different ways, as one of five social psychology experiments.

    What they found The framing effect appeared, and the effects were of similar size for smartphone and smart speaker users. All effects were significant at the 95% level. This matches the original finding that the wording of a problem shifts people's choices.

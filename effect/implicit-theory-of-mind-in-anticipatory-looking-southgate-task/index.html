Implicit Theory of Mind in anticipatory looking (Southgate task)

Southgate et al., 2007

The claim

Toddlers look ahead to where an actor will reach, based on what the actor wrongly believes, not what is true.

In the original authors' words: “For our second measure, we coded the amount of time each infant focused on each window. As the infants were familiarized to a delay of 1,750 ms between the onset of illumination and the opening of a window, we coded only the first 1,750 ms after onset of…”

Also filed as: False-belief action anticipation in 25-month-old children · False-belief anticipatory looking in the Southgate task · False-belief looking in the Southgate task · False-belief tracking guides anticipatory looking · Theory of Mind · Two-year-olds anticipate action from another person’s false belief

How the original study workedChanges the task or its wordingmeasured as Speed, accuracy or memoryBabies and toddlers

What people did

Toddlers watched an actor on a computer screen while an eyetracker recorded where they looked. The actor's action could only be predicted if the toddler kept in mind what the actor wrongly believed.

What the original found

The 25-month-olds spent almost twice as long looking at the correct window as at the incorrect one.

In the original authors’ words: “For our second measure, we coded the amount of time each infant focused on each window. As the infants were familiarized to a delay of 1,750 ms between the onset of illumination and the opening of a window, we coded only the first 1,750 ms after onset of illumination on the test trial.”

What happened when others tried it

7 attempts, oldest first
  1. 2017
    Did not find itKulke et al.

    What they did Two independent research labs ran three studies that tried to repeat two earlier implicit looking-time tasks (the Southgate et al. 2007 task and the Surian and Geraci 2012 task), in which where people look is taken to show whether they expect someone to act on a false belief. The studies tested children, adults and elderly adults, and also checked whether results on the two tasks correlated with each other.

    What they found The original findings were not replicated in children or in elderly adults, and were only partially replicated in adults. Results on the two tasks did not correlate, which the authors say puts into question whether the two tasks tap the same capacity of an implicit Theory of Mind. They conclude that results from implicit Theory of Mind tasks should be treated with caution.

  2. 2017
    Did not find itWiesmann et al.Cognitive Development

    What they did The same children were tested several times, from age 2 to age 4, on an established false belief task. It used anticipatory looking: where a child looks shows where they expect an agent to reach for an object, when the agent falsely believes the object is in a certain place.

    What they found Children anticipated correctly only by the age of 4 years, and performed at chance at the ages of 2 and 3 years. The original study had found correct anticipation in 25-month-olds. Correct anticipation appeared only when the agent falsely believed the object to be in its last rather than a previous location. The authors say this points to the fragility of early belief-related action anticipation before age 4, when children start passing traditional false belief tasks.

  3. 2018
    Did not find itKulke & RakoczyJournal of Cognition and Development

    What they did Sixty-seven 4- to 5-year-old children watched modified versions of two videos built to test whether children expect a person to act on a false belief. Verbal narration was added to the original videos, and the children's anticipatory looking (where they looked before the person acted) was measured.

    What they found The original looking patterns could still not be replicated. In one task there was no improvement, and in the other a slight improvement was seen. The authors conclude that adding verbal narration does not necessarily improve replicability, which suggests either that these looking measures may not be sensitive enough to show an early understanding of false belief, or that other factors are crucial for successful replications.

  4. 2018
    Did not find itKulke et al.Psychological Science

    What they did Adults took part in a preregistered attempt to repeat four earlier anticipatory-looking tasks, which track where people look to show whether they expect an actor to act on a belief about where an object is. The team used the original stimuli and procedures, including those of the Southgate et al. (2007) study, which had tested 25-month-olds. They also tested whether the paradigms' results matched one another.

    What they found The authors report that they failed to replicate the full pattern seen in the Southgate et al. (2007) studies. Of the four paradigms, only one was reliably replicated, and in a second set of studies it was no longer replicated once confounds were removed. There were no correlations between the paradigms. The authors conclude that findings from these anticipatory-looking false-belief tasks seem less reliable and valid than previously assumed.

  5. 2018
    Did not find itSchuwerk et al.Royal Society Open Science

    What they did The team tested children's anticipatory looking, meaning where they look in advance when watching an agent whose belief is false. In a second, preregistered experiment, 78 children aged 25 months took part, and 115 adults were tested too.

    What they found In a first experiment, which also included two manipulations, the authors had already failed to replicate the original finding in 2- to 3-year-olds (48 children). In the second, the 25-month-olds did not show the original pattern, though the adults did. In all samples, a surprisingly high number of participants did not correctly anticipate the agent's action during the familiarization phase. Applying the original studies' criteria therefore led to massive exclusion rates, which the authors say strongly limits how the test-phase findings can be interpreted; only 17 of the 78 children met the final analysis criteria. The authors discuss both the reliability of their replication attempts and the replicability of non-verbal anticipatory looking paradigms of implicit false belief sensitivity in general.

  6. 2019
    Found part of itKulkeRoyal Society Open Science

    What they did Adults watched computer videos in which a person acts on a belief that is false, while eye-tracking recorded where they looked in advance of the person's action. The team adapted a task first run with 25-month-olds, using more realistic stimuli and controlling for potential confounds, in two preregistered studies.

    What they found In the first study, in the removal conditions, only the FB1 condition was significantly replicated, while the more controlled FB2 condition was at chance. Across the studies, previous results could not be replicated, and the looking pattern remained largely compatible with more parsimonious explanations than automatic Theory of Mind.

  7. 2021
    Did not find itKampis et al.Royal Society Open Science

    What they did One hundred and sixty 24- to 26-month-olds were tested in two places, Copenhagen and Budapest, using the original stimuli, procedure and analyses as closely as possible. Two of the original authors took part. The team tracked whether each infant looked to the spot where an agent was about to act.

    What they found Only roughly half of the infants looked to the location of the agent's impending action when the agent's beliefs did not matter, and a similar number did so when the agent held a false belief. The authors found no evidence for action anticipation, unlike the original. They conclude the paradigm does not reliably elicit action prediction and so cannot assess false-belief understanding in 2-year-olds, while saying the results do not show whether infants have theory of mind.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    A two-lab direct replication attempt of Southgate, Senju and Csibra (2007)

    “We found no evidence for action anticipation: only roughly half of the infants looked to the location of an agent's impending action when action prediction did not require taking into account the agent's beliefs”

Action anticipation did not occur even when belief attribution was unnecessary, so this specifically undermines the original anticipatory-looking paradigm as evidence of false-belief understanding; it does not show that 2-year-olds lack theory of mind.

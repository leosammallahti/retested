Cleanliness Priming

Schnall et al., 2008

The claim

Having cleanliness on your mind makes you judge other people's moral wrongdoings as less wrong than you otherwise would.

In the original authors' words: “participants gave lower ratings after the cleanliness priming (M = 4.98, SD = 1.26) than they did after the neutral priming (M = 5.81, SD = 1.47), F(1, 38) = 3.63, prep 5.90, np 2 = .09”

Also filed as: Moral priming (cleanliness)

How the original study workedSmall cue that spills overmeasured as What people said or rated

What people did

Study 1

People did a task meant to bring the idea of cleanliness to mind, while others did a neutral task. Then everyone rated how morally wrong certain actions were.

What the original found

People who had cleanliness on their minds rated the actions as less wrong than people who did the neutral task.

In the original authors’ words: “participants gave lower ratings after the cleanliness priming (M = 4.98, SD = 1.26) than they did after the neutral priming (M = 5.81, SD = 1.47), F(1, 38) = 3.63, prep 5.90, np 2 = .09”

Study 2

People watched a disgusting film clip. Afterward, some washed their hands and others did not. Then everyone rated how wrong several moral wrongdoings were.

What the original found

On average, the people who washed their hands rated the wrongdoings as less wrong than the people who did not wash.

In the original authors’ words: “We computed the mean composite of all six moral vignettes and predicted that participants who washed their hands following the disgusting film clip would make less severe judgments than would participants who did not wash their hands.   3. 2014
    Found itHuangFrontiers in Psychology

    What they did In an online replication, 214 participants worked on a priming task with either cleanliness-related or neutral material, then rated how wrong certain moral actions were. The team looked at how much effort people put into the priming material, and compared ratings for those who gave low (yet still acceptable) effort.

    What they found Among participants with low (yet still acceptable) response effort, cleanliness priming led to more lenient moral judgments than neutral priming, the same pattern as the original finding. A second online experiment (440 people, repeated with 436) set effort on purpose: the effect appeared when people were told to do the priming task quickly, and there was no significant difference when they were told to do their best. The authors note an earlier direct replication with much larger samples had not found this.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “An online replication (Study 1, N = 214) provided evidence that, when participants exerted low (yet still acceptable) levels of response effort to the experimental material, cleanliness priming led to more lenient moral judgments than neutral priming.”

  4. 2014
    Did not find itJohnson et al.Social Psychology

    What they did Using materials supplied by the original authors, 126 people took part in a repeat of the second experiment, in which people wash their hands after experiencing disgust and then judge how wrong certain moral actions are.

    What they found The team did not find evidence that physical cleanliness reduced the severity of moral judgments. The size of the effect they measured was much smaller than in the original experiment. The authors say their sample was large enough to give high power to detect the original effect size, and conclude that the population effect is probably substantially smaller than the original estimate, so research on cleanliness and morality needs large samples to detect subtle effects.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “We did not find evidence that physical cleanliness reduced the severity of moral judgments using samples sizes that provided over .99 power to detect the original effect sizes.”

  6. 2014
    Found itArbesfeld et al.PsychFileDrawer.org

    What they did Participants were randomly put into two groups. Both groups unscrambled sets of jumbled words into sentences, as much time as they needed. One group's sets included cleanliness-related words and the control group's did not. Everyone then read the same six moral dilemmas used in the original study and rated each one on a 0 to 9 scale, from "perfectly fine" to "extremely immoral".

    What they found The group primed with cleanliness words rated the dilemmas as less severe on average than the control group, a significant difference on a one-tailed test. This is the same direction the original study reported, and the project classed the outcome as a successful replication.

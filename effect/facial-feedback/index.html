Facial Feedback

Strack et al., 1988

The claim

Moving your face into a smile, even without being told to smile, makes cartoons seem funnier and you feel more amused.

In the original authors' words: “Subjects reported more intense humor responses when cartoons were presented under facilitating conditions than under inhibiting conditions”

Also filed as: Facial feedback changes cartoon humor ratings

How the original study workedSmall cue that spills overmeasured as What people said or rated

What people did

People held a pen in their mouth while looking at cartoons. Some held it in a way that made smiling hard, and others in a way that made smiling easier, though nobody was told to smile.

What the original found

People who held the pen the smile-friendly way said the cartoons were funnier than those who held it the smile-blocking way.

In the original authors’ words: “Subjects reported more intense humor responses when cartoons were presented under facilitating conditions than under inhibiting conditions”

What happened when others tried it

8 attempts, oldest first
  1. 2014
    Found part of itDzokoto et al.The Journal of General Psychology

    What they did Participants from two universities in Ghana first rated how much attention they pay to their own emotions. They then rated cartoons while randomly assigned to a smiling, frowning, or neutral facial condition.

    What they found Participants with low Attention to Emotion scores showed the usual facial feedback effect, rating cartoons as funnier when smiling than when frowning. The effect was not present in individuals with high Attention to Emotion. Attention to emotion was this study's addition, so the original pattern reappeared for part of the sample.

  3. 2016
    Did not find itWagenmakers et al.Perspectives on Psychological Science

    What they did Seventeen research teams each repeated the first study from the 1988 original, following the same agreed protocol. Participants held a pen in their mouth, either with their teeth (inducing a "smile") or with their lips (inducing a "pout"), and rated how funny cartoons were on a 10-point scale.

    What they found Combining the 17 studies, the funniness ratings in the "smile" and "pout" conditions differed by 0.03 units, and the range of plausible values around that difference included zero. The original study had reported a difference of 0.82 units, so this is not the same result as the original.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

  6. 2018
    Found itMarsh et al.Emotion

    What they did Over nine semesters, more than 400 undergraduates took part in a classroom demonstration. Some held a pen in their teeth and others held a pen in their lips while they rated cartoons.

    What they found The authors report robust evidence in favor of the facial feedback hypothesis, the idea that the expression on a person's face influences how they feel. This matches the pattern in the original study.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Results yielded robust evidence in favor of the facial feedback hypothesis.”
    paper
  7. 2018
    Found part of itNoah et al.Journal of Personality and Social Psychology

    What they did A new group of participants did the pen-in-mouth cartoon rating task again. Some took part with a video camera present and some without one.

    What they found Without a camera, the team found a significant facial-feedback effect, as in the original. With the camera present, the effect was eliminated. The authors suggest that feeling observed reduces how much people rely on internal cues, which may explain why an earlier 17-laboratory replication, where participants were told a camera would monitor them, found no support for the effect.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “The results revealed a significant facial-feedback effect in the absence of a camera, which was eliminated in the camera's presence.”
    paper
  8. 2020
    Did not find itVesker et al.

    What they did The study tested whether the embodiment effect of the facial feedback hypothesis, supported by a key pen-in-the-mouth study that several labs have since failed to replicate, appears in a simplified emotional categorization task, and whether it appears in children. Children and adults sorted faces and words into positive or negative as quickly as they could. While sorting, they held a straw in the mouth so that it produced a smile or a frown, or they held a positive or negative body posture. The bodily state either matched or did not match the positive or negative feeling of the face or word being sorted.

    What they found The team found no significant differences between matching and non-matching conditions, in either children or adults. They suggest that these embodiment effects either do not significantly affect sorting by positive or negative feeling, or are not strong enough to be detected with the sample size they had.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, and read against it by another AI model.

    “Our results did not show any significant differences between the congruent and incongruent conditions in either children or adults.”

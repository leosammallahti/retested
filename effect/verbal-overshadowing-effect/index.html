Verbal overshadowing effect

Schooler & Engstler-Schooler, 1990

The claim

Describing a face in words makes you worse at picking that person out later.

Also filed as: Verbal Overshadowing Effect · verbal overshadowing effect

How the original study workedChanges the task or its wordingmeasured as Speed, accuracy or memory

What people did

People watched a video that included a salient person. Later, some described that person's face and others did not. Then everyone tried to recognize the face.

What the original found

People who had described the face did worse at recognizing it.

  2. 2014
    Found itAlogna et al.Perspectives on Psychological Science

    What they did Participants watched a video of a simulated bank robbery. Some then described the robber in words, while others listed U.S. states and capitals, and everyone tried to pick the robber from a lineup. Two sets of preregistered replications differed in timing: in one the description came right after the video, in the other 20 minutes later.

    What they found With the description right after the video, describers were 4% less likely to pick the robber than the control group. With the 20-minute delay, they were 16% less likely. The authors call this a robust verbal overshadowing effect, strongly influenced by the timing of the tasks. The original reported a 25% drop.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “These findings reveal a robust verbal overshadowing effect that is strongly influenced by the relative timing of the tasks.”

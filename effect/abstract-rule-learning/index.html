Abstract rule learning

Marcus et al., 1999

The claim

After hearing made-up sentences that follow one pattern, babies notice and look longer at sentences that follow a different pattern.

In the original authors' words: “7-month-old infants attend longer to sentences with unfamiliar structures than to sentences with familiar structures”

Also filed as: Infant Rule Learning (Speech) · Infant algebraic rule learning · Rule learning effect in infants

How the original study workedChanges the task or its wordingmeasured as Speed, accuracy or memoryBabies and toddlers

What people did

Seven-month-old babies listened to made-up sentences that all followed one pattern. Then they heard new sentences, some with the same pattern and some with a different one.

What the original found

The babies listened longer to the sentences with the unfamiliar pattern.

What happened when others tried it

5 attempts, oldest first
  1. 2012
    Found itGervainJournal of Child Language

    What they did Infants aged 0;7 (seven months) took part in two experiments with three-syllable nonsense phrases. In one, phrases with an immediate repeat (ABB, like "wo fe fe") were compared with random phrases with no repeat (ABC). In the other, phrases with a repeat separated by a syllable (ABA, like "wo fe wo") were compared with the same random phrases (ABC). The original had compared the two repetition types with each other, a design in which learning only one type was enough to succeed.

    What they found The infants successfully discriminated both kinds of repetition phrase from the random ones, but showed no spontaneous preference for either of them. The authors note this runs contrary to some previous proposals.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “We show that, contrary to some previous proposals, infants aged 0 ; 7 successfully discriminate both repetition types from random controls, but show no spontaneous preference for either of them.”
    paperfree preprint
  2. 2014
    Found part of itKovácsLanguage Learning

    What they did Seven-month-old infants heard syllable patterns that were 50% noisy. In one experiment the patterns followed AAB and ABA structures, where repeated syllables sit next to each other (AAB) or are separated by a different syllable (ABA). In another experiment the ABA patterns were paired with ABC structures, where all three syllables differ.

    What they found In the second experiment, the infants generalized only the AAB patterns. In the third experiment, they could learn the ABA patterns when these were paired with ABC structures. So the original rule-learning result appeared for AAB but, for ABA, only in the ABC pairing.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “In Experiment 2, 7-month-olds heard AAB and ABA patterns and generalized only the AAB patterns. However, in Experiment 3 they could learn the ABA patterns when these were paired with ABC structures.”
    paper
  3. 2019
    Found itKabdebonProceedings of the National Academy of Sciences

    What they did In three experiments, 150 5-month-old infants heard speech triplets built on a pattern of where a syllable repeated (ABA or AAB). Each pattern was followed by an arbitrary label, such as a fish picture after ABA words and a lion after AAB words. After a brief learning phase, infants heard novel words while their brain activity was recorded with EEG.

    What they found Brain responses to the novel words showed that infants built expectations about the upcoming label from the triplet structure and were surprised when it was incongruent. Infants also generalized to novel trials in which the label came first, and the authors conclude that they instantly inferred a bidirectional mapping between the triplet structure and the label, beyond conditioned associations. This conceptual replication used brain recordings in younger infants; the original measured how long 7-month-olds attended to sentences.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “After a brief learning phase, EEG responses to novel words revealed that infants built expectations about the upcoming label based on the triplet structure and were surprised when it happened to be incongruent.”
    paperfree text
  4. 2022
    Did not find itGeambașu et al.Developmental Science

    What they did Four labs tested 96 seven-month-old babies. The babies first heard a stretch of syllables arranged in one of two patterns (ABA or ABB), then heard new syllables arranged in a pattern that matched or did not match what they had heard. The team used the original study's patterns and stimuli, and measured how long each baby looked (Headturn Preference Paradigm).

    What they found The babies showed no difference in looking time between test patterns that matched the pattern they had heard and those that did not. This did not replicate the original finding.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Our results did not replicate the original findings: infants showed no difference in looking time between test patterns consistent or inconsistent with the familiarization pattern they were exposed to.”

  5. 2026
    Did not find itVisser et al.Developmental Science

    What they did In a registered conceptual replication of the original study, thirty laboratories tested 839 infants aged 5 to 12 months (650 were analyzed after exclusions). The infants heard syllable sequences following a pattern, then heard new sequences that were either consistent or inconsistent with that pattern. The researchers measured how long the infants looked during the consistent and inconsistent test trials.

    What they found Looking times did not differ significantly between consistent and inconsistent trials, so the team found no evidence of rule-learning, unlike the original study. Age, whether infants were mono- or multilingual, and the experimental paradigm showed no significant effects. In 30 of the 31 samples, individual labs also found no evidence of rule-learning. A Bayesian analysis indicated strong evidence for a null effect, and the results did not differ under different choices in processing the data. The authors say the finding raises fundamental issues about rule-learning.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “The current study therefore finds no evidence for rule-learning”

Why results may differ

only what a source states or the numbers show
  • The original tested 16 people; the attempt with a recorded size tested more (96). — FReD
  • Gervain 2012: Independent same-age speech-syllable conceptual replication of repetition-pattern learning. — reviewer’s note
  • Gervain 2012: Compares each grammar with random ABC controls rather than Marcus’s unfamiliar-vs-familiar ABA/ABB contrast; both repetition types discriminated. — reviewer’s note
  • Kabdebon 2019: Independent conceptual replication in younger infants: novel ABA/AAB speech triplets generalized to predict visual labels, measured with EEG rather than original looking-time preference. — reviewer’s note
  • Visser et al. 2026: Registered multi-lab conceptual replication with 650 analyzed infants; the original is explicitly named. — reviewer’s note

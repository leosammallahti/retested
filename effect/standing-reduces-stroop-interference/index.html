Standing reduces Stroop interference

2 original papers

The claim

Standing up instead of sitting down helps people ignore distracting information, so they slow down less when a color word clashes with its ink.

In the original authors' words: “The magnitude of the interference was modulated by posture (posture × congruency interaction: F(2, 26) = 4.73, p = .018, ηp² = .27). Specifically, all of the observed Stroop interference occurred in the seated posture (mean difference between incongruent and…”

Tested the original paper

Stand by Your Stroop: Standing Up Enhances Selective Attention and Cognitive Control

Rosenbaum et al. · 2017 · Psychological Science · doi.org/10.1177/0956797617721270

  1. 2019
    Found itSmith et al.Attention, Perception, &amp; Psychophysics

    What they did Forty-five people did a Stroop color-word task, which measures how much it slows you down when a word's meaning clashes with the color it is printed in. They did it both standing and sitting. They also did a task-switching task and a visual search task in the two postures.

    What they found In the Stroop task, the slowing caused by clashing words was markedly reduced when people stood, consistent with the original finding. The authors also report a reduction in switch costs and slower search rates in visual search when people stood. They say the results suggest that the postural demands associated with standing enhance cognitive control, revealing broad connections between body posture and cognitive mechanisms.

    What they did Five experiments, run at two institutions, had people do the Stroop task: name the ink color of a color word when the word's meaning matches the color or clashes with it.   3. 2022
    Did not find itStraub et al.Acta Psychologica

    What they did Participants did a Color Stroop task, in which people respond to the ink colour of words that either match or clash with that colour, while standing and while sitting. The team ran two versions of this test of the original idea, and also combined results across studies in a meta-analysis.

    What they found Both experiments showed the usual Stroop effect, but posture did not change it, even when the analysis controlled for differences between individual participants. This does not match the original finding that standing improved performance. An exploratory Bayesian analysis found strong evidence against an interaction between posture and the Stroop effect in both experiments, and in the meta-analysis the overall effect of posture on the Stroop effect was compatible with no effect. The authors say their results question whether standing modulates the Stroop effect in Color Stroop tasks and point out limitations of the influence of body posture on cognitive control tasks.

  6. 2025
    Did not find itBusch et al.Scientific Reports

    What they did Thirty-six healthy adults did the Stroop task (naming a colour while ignoring a distracting word) and a second task, the Navon task, which tests conflict between spatial locations. The team took account of each person's BMI, physical activity level and cardiac measures, and compared standing with the other posture.

    What they found The team found evidence against an effect of standing posture on Stroop interference, which differs from the original finding. In the Navon task, standing significantly improved spatial conflict processing, and the authors report this effect was fully mediated by enhanced physiological arousal, indexed by reduced heart rate variability. They say the findings challenge the idea of a unified attentional control system, revealing that posture influences conflict processing differently depending on the task.

Tested the original paper

Standing enhances cognitive control and alters visual search

Smith et al. · 2019 · Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics · doi.org/10.3758/s13414-019-01723-6

Study experiment 1

People named the ink color of color words, some spelling a different color than the ink and others the same, once while sitting and once while standing. When seated, people were slower on the mismatched words. When standing, they were not.

Study experiment 3

People searched displays of four or eight items for a target, once while sitting and once while standing. People searched more slowly while standing than while sitting.

  1. 2022
    Did not find itStraub et al.Acta Psychologica

    What they did Two experiments, run as conceptual replications, had people do a Color Stroop task, where you respond to the color of written color words. The researchers compared performance across body postures, standing versus sitting. 38 people were tested.

    What they found The usual Stroop effect appeared in both experiments, but body posture did not change it. This held even when the analysis controlled for differences between individual participants. That differs from the original finding that standing reduced the Stroop effect.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Replication experiments showed typical congruency effects in the Color Stroop task, but failed to find any influence of posture on the Stroop effect even when we subjected data to a more sensitive analysis that controlled for individual variances between participants.”
    38 peopletested Experiment 1no author in common with the originalsame paper is filed against more than one originalpaperfree text
  2. 2022
    Did not find itCaron et al.Experimental Psychology

    What they did The team ran close copies of the original three experiments: a Stroop task (naming the ink color of color words), a task-switching task and a visual search task. Each used larger groups of people than the original work, and the aim was to see whether standing led to better performance than sitting.

    What they found The effects of posture were quite limited in magnitude, only a fraction of the size of the original effects. The authors say the first experiment's Stroop result matches two other recent replications, which reported no meaningful influence of posture on the Stroop effect. They say the research provides further converging evidence that postural influences on cognition do not appear to be as robust as was initially reported.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

    “The results from our experiments revealed that, in contrast to Smith et al., the postural interactions were quite limited in magnitude in addition to being only a fraction of the size of the original effects.”

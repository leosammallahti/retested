Emotion intensity shifts regulation choice toward distraction

Sheppes et al., 2011

The claim

People prefer to rethink mildly upsetting situations and to distract themselves from intensely upsetting ones.

In the original authors' words: “Participants chose to employ reappraisal more frequently in low-intensity trials (76.3%, 95% CI: [66.3, 86.3]) compared to distraction in high-intensity trials (70.7%, 95% CI: [60.7, 80.7]), with a significant difference observed, F (1, 19) = 47.54, p <…”

How the original study workedChanges the task or its wordingmeasured as What people actually did or chose

What people did

People faced mild or intense upsetting pictures or electric shocks. Each time they chose between two ways to handle their feelings: thinking about the situation differently, or distracting themselves.

What the original found

People preferred thinking about it differently for the mild situations and distracting themselves for the intense ones.

In the original authors’ words: “Participants chose to employ reappraisal more frequently in low-intensity trials (76.3%, 95% CI: [66.3, 86.3]) compared to distraction in high-intensity trials (70.7%, 95% CI: [60.7, 80.7]), with a significant difference observed, F (1, 19) = 47.54, p < .000001, partial eta-square = .71”

What happened when others tried it

5 attempts, oldest first
  1. 2022
    Found itNardelli et al.

    What they did 128 people from the general population (not clinical patients) completed two tasks in which they chose how to handle their emotions. One task repeated the original design, and the other added more than two strategies to choose from. The team also looked at how psychosis-proneness related to people's choices.

    What they found People favoured disengagement strategies when the emotional intensity was high and engagement strategies when it was low, the same pattern as the original. Psychosis-proneness seemed to be associated with difficulties in adapting to the emotional context.

    Found itNardelli et al.Collabra: Psychology

    What they did 128 people from the general population (not patients) did two tasks. In each, they chose a strategy for handling their emotions under two conditions, one with high emotional intensity and one with low. One task repeated the original design; the other offered more than two strategies to choose from. The team also explored the role of psychosis-proneness in these choices.

    What they found People favoured disengagement strategies when the emotional intensity was high and engagement strategies when it was low. This is the same pattern the original study reported. Psychosis-proneness seemed to be associated with difficulties in adapting to the emotional context.

    Found itBoyce et al.Royal Society Open Science

    What they did In one of the 176 replications in this paper, all from students in a graduate-level methods course, 17 people took part in a repeat of the emotion-regulation choice study, in which people choose between rethinking a situation and distracting themselves.

    What they found Across the paper's replications, 49% were judged successful. Of the 136 where effect sizes could be numerically compared, 46% had results within the range predicted from the original outcome, versus the expected 95%.

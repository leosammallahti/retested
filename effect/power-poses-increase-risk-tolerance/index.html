Power poses increase risk tolerance

Carney et al., 2010

The claim

Holding wide, open poses for about a minute makes people more willing to take financial gambles than holding closed, shrunken poses.

In the original authors' words: “High-power posers experienced elevations in testosterone, decreases in cortisol, and increased feelings of power and tolerance for risk; low-power posers exhibited the opposite pattern.”

Also filed as: Expansive posture increases risky choice

How the original study workedSmall cue that spills overmeasured as What people actually did or choseMeasured in the body, brain, hormones or genes

What people did

People held two body poses for about a minute each. Some took wide, open, take-up-space poses, and others took closed, shrunken-in poses. Afterward they could take a gamble or play it safe.

What the original found

More of the open-pose group took the gamble than the closed-pose group.

In the original authors’ words: “As hypothesized, high-power poses caused an increase in testosterone compared with low-power poses, which caused a decrease in testosterone, F(1, 39) = 4.29, p < .05; r = .34”

A plain-words account of the experiment, written by an AI model and read against its paper before it was used.

  3. 2026
    Found part of itWainio-Theberge & ArmonyBritish Journal of Psychology

    What they did Participants did a 30-trial balloon task, a risk-taking game, while their posture was shaped by the environment rather than by instructed poses: table height led to an upright or a stooped posture. The team also measured mood.

    What they found There was no significant difference between the posture groups overall. But the groups differed in how their risk-taking changed across the repeated trials. This study was not preregistered. The original found that posing in expansive postures raised tolerance for risk; here the effect showed up over repeated trials, and against a separately recruited control group only the expansive posture differed.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “There was a simple main effect of trial (β = 0.37, χ² = 39.5, p < .0001), as well as an interaction between trial and group (β = 0.26, χ² = 5.02, p = .03); the simple main effect of group did not reach significance”

  3. MixedWainio-Theberge & Armony, 2026British Journal of Psychology

    Manipulating posture implicitly through environmental constraints influences mood and risk-taking behaviour

    “There was a simple main effect of trial (β = 0.37, χ² = 39.5, p < .0001), as well as an interaction between trial and group (β = 0.26, χ² = 5.02, p = .03); the simple main effect of group did not reach significance”

    Conceptual retest of Carney et al. (2010) posture→risky-choice claim using incidental table-height induced upright/stooped posture, rather than instructed poses, and a 30-trial balloon task rather than the original one-shot gamble. n=198 enrolled, BART analysis n=195. Main group effect p=.06; group×trial p=.03 and modeled risk propensity group effect p=.03. Compared with separately recruited standard-setup control (n=133), expansive differed p=.01 and contractive did not. Thus mixed evidence for a simple main effect but support for risk-taking over repeated trials. Authors explicitly say study not preregistered. Carney/Cuddy/Yap do not overlap Wainio-Theberge/Armony.

Risky choice framing (Asian disease problem)

Tversky & Kahneman, 1981

The claim

People tend to pick the sure option when disease-fighting choices are described as lives saved, and to gamble when described as deaths.

In the original authors' words: “Choices involving gains are often risk-averse, while choices involving losses are often risk-seeking. For example, in Problem 1 (N = 152), 72% of participants chose the safe option (Program A: 200 lives saved, p < .05), compared to 28% who chose the risky…”

Also filed as: Gain versus loss framing changes risk choice · Gain versus loss framing in life and death choices · Risky Choice Framing · Risky choice framing effect · Unusual Disease Problem · Unusual disease problem

How the original study workedChanges the task or its wordingmeasured as What people actually did or chose

What people did

People read about an outbreak expected to kill 600 people and chose between a sure outcome and a gamble. For some the outcomes were described as people saved, and for others as people dying.

What the original found

When it was about lives saved people picked the sure option, and when it was about lives lost they picked the gamble.

    What they did In this replication of the framing-of-decisions study, 6,271 people were tested. Each chose between programs for a problem, and one group saw the options described as gains while the other saw them described as losses.

  4. 2016
    Found itZhen & YuScientific Reports

    What they did People read made-up scenarios about a disease outbreak and a hostage situation, with the same options worded either as lives saved or as lives lost. Each person answered repeated trials, choosing between a sure option and a gamble. In all, 151 people's answers were analyzed.

    What they found In the scenarios worded as losses, people picked the gambling option 57% of the time, compared with 46% when the same options were worded positively. The authors report this difference as significant, the same direction of effect as in the original study. The paper's main finding, though, is that this framing measure and a second classic one agreed poorly with each other.

    What they did In variations of Tversky and Kahneman's Asian Disease problem, participants made choices about public health programs, picking between a sure option and a risky option.     What they did In the second study, 584 participants were recruited and given the Unusual Disease Problem, a choice between a certain option and a risky option. One group saw the options described as gains, and the other saw them described as losses.

  11. 2021
    Found itOtterbring et al.Safety Science

    What they did Across two experiments, people read about an outbreak of either a real disease or a hypothetical one. The information was framed either positively or negatively, and people said how willing they were to take risks and to comply with preventive health behaviors. Their level of emotionality was also measured.

    What they found The authors report that the classic Asian Disease Problem effect was replicated regardless of disease type. People were less willing to take risks when information was framed positively and more willing when it was framed negatively, though they were generally more risk-averse in real pandemics. People high in emotionality were more willing to comply when information was framed positively, but only for a real disease.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract and the authors’ own words, and read against them by another AI model.

  13. 2021
    Found itRachev et al.PLoS ONE

    What they did From March to May 2020, a large online survey of 88,181 people from 47 countries gave participants the Disease problem, a choice between options for dealing with a disease outbreak, worded in different ways. It also measured their perceived stress, their concerns over coronavirus and their trust in the government's efforts to handle it.

    What they found The wording of the options changed people's choices, as in the original. Risk aversion and the framing effect were larger than under typical circumstances. Stress and concern were linked to a bigger framing effect, but not to risk aversion. Trust in government was linked to neither. Choices varied across nations. The framing effect was unrelated to reported compliance with safety measures, which the authors suggest limits how well framing in public messages may work.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “risk aversion and the framing effect in our study were larger than under typical circumstances.”

  15. 2022
    Found itLiFrontiers in Psychology

    What they did 63 older and 66 younger adults chose between a sure option and a gamble in a fund investment scenario adapted from the "Asian Disease Problem". The options were worded either as gains or as losses, and some participants were watched by images of eyes. Eye tracking recorded where participants looked while deciding.

    What they found Both younger and older adults tended to seek risk more in the loss wording than in the gain wording, the same framing pattern as in the original. Watching eyes was a separate factor: it did not change younger adults' risk seeking but decreased their framing effect, while older adults tended to keep gambling whether the options were framed as gains or losses.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “The results found that both younger and older adults tend to seek risk in the loss frame than in the gain frame (i.e., framing effect).”

  16. 2022
    Found itIm & ChenJournal of Behavioral Decision Making

    What they did Using data from a global survey collected across 49 countries (102,830 respondents), the team gave people a version of the classic Asian Disease scenario. Respondents chose between a safe option (a known number of lives saved or lost) and a risky option (a probability of saving lives). The problem was framed either as lives saved or as lives lost.

    What they found Respondents were more likely to pick the safe option when the problem was framed as lives saved than as lives lost, and this framing effect appeared in 100% of the countries examined, matching the original finding. Societal collectivism was linked to a lower tendency to choose the safe option in both frames, and partly to smaller framing effects at the country level.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “was replicated in 100% of the countries examined”

  17. 2022
    Found part of itXu & HuangFrontiers in Psychology

    What they did Across two studies, people chose between rescue plans in the classic Asian Disease problem, where the same outcomes are described either as lives saved (gain) or lives lost (loss). The researchers also varied how spatially far away the event was.

    What they found The gain/loss framing effect appeared in the ordinary and nearby conditions. At far spatial distance, people increased their preference for the risky option under the gain frame, and the framing effect disappeared. The results were mixed for the idea that the framing reversal holds regardless of context: it held in the ordinary and nearby conditions but not at far distance.

    Written by an AI model from the authors’ own words and a reviewer’s note, and read against them by another AI model.

    “Distant spatial distance lead individuals to increase their preference for risk-seeking under the gain frame, which leads to the disappearance of the framing effect.”
    paperfree text
  18. 2023
    Found itPeterson & Tollefson

    What they did In a survey run through a Qualtrics panel, 1209 customers of Utah utilities answered a version of the Asian disease problem rewritten around deaths attributed to climate change. The question was set up in a gain frame and a loss frame, and the team compared responses between the two frames.

    What they found The team reported that the gain/loss framing effect was present. It also held when responses were split by age, gender, race, marital status, income, educational attainment, political preference, living status, household size, years at current residence, and energy-saving preference, which the authors describe as matching the original Asian disease problem. They recommend using the framing effects of the Asian disease problem in energy- and climate-related programs, initiatives and research, and believe this framing could spur action to mitigate climate change.

    Written by an AI model from the later paper’s abstract, and read against it by another AI model.

    “we confirmed the existence of the gain/loss framing effect”
    paperfree text

Outside the record

1,211 replications nobody counted

The counted record combines FORRT's data with individually reviewed Retested additions. Searching OpenAlex for psychology replications outside that record turns up 1,211 more, 547 of them matched to the specific study they re-test, across 498 distinct original papers. 284 state an outcome.

None of this is verified, and none of it counts. A language model decided each paper is a replication, a machine matched it to an original, and the outcome was read off an abstract. No figure on this page contributes to any rate, verdict or tally anywhere else on the site. It is here to ask one question: does the record that was curated look like the record that was not? Help decide the individual cases in the review queue.

The uncounted replications succeed more often, and the two intervals barely meet. That is a difference worth explaining rather than a result to report, and the first explanation to rule out was ours: a model reading outcomes off abstracts might simply find a plain success easier to recognise than a hedged failure. So 29 of these verdicts were read one by one against the paper's own abstract. The ones marked failed were right 13 times in 16, the ones marked successful 9 in 10, the ones marked mixed 3 in 3 — and not one of the 29 was read as the opposite of what the paper reports. The mistakes are all abstracts that settle nothing either way, and they fall on both sides. Re-weighting every verdict in this row by what that reading found moves it from 44.4% to 41.7%: slightly further from the curated record, not closer. So over-read success does not explain this gap. What is left is the other explanation, and it is untested: curation is not random, and a database assembled around the replication crisis may hold more of the famous failures. Until somebody has reviewed enough of these to show that, treat this row as a question rather than a finding.

In FORRT's curated data, replications sharing an author with the original succeed 70% of the time against 38% for independent teams. These finds were gathered by a different route, matched by a different method, and coded by a different reader — and show the same split. That is the best evidence on this page that the pipeline behind it is measuring something real.

715 of these finds are preprints or OSF deposits rather than journal articles — student projects, class replications, multi-site teaching collaborations. They are most of what a catalogue built from journal metadata cannot see, which is a large part of why they were missing.

What would have to be true

before any of this counts

Each of these finds rests on three machine judgements, and each was measured by reading cases one by one rather than asserted. Whether the paper is a replication at all: right 86% of the time on the strong search tier against the owner's 70 hand labels, 50% on the weak tier. Whether it was matched to the right original: 96% for a library match, 91% for an OpenAlex match, 89% for a match against the paper's own reference list, and lower for the weaker bands. What the outcome was: read off an abstract, with the quote kept beside it so a reader can check.

Multiply those and a given row is likelier right than wrong, which is enough to put in front of a reviewer and nowhere near enough to publish as a rate. The review queue is where that gets fixed, one paper at a time, and 107 of the candidates in it would change a verdict on this site if accepted.
